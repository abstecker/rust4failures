Rust for Failures

Cards

The game is No Limit Texas Hold'em, specifically an exploration of playing cards, and how they can be digitally programmed to reflect the different perspectives games of poker.

At first I thought that I would try to organize this, weaving in theory into the code as we code together, demonstrating a planned, rational journey together. I find it extremely inspiring how developers can plan out what they want to accomplish with clear design documents for games or complex systems, and I've certainly written many of the latter in a professional capacity, except that's not how I approached the work that is the foundation of this book, and it's not how I want to write about it.

I guess the formal definition of the area of exploration is called Domain-driven design, a term coined by Eric Evans in 2003 in a book by the same name. The concepts in that book had a profound effect on me when I was evolving into a reckless startup developer to a consultant for various companies.

But there are aspects of DDD that I will not be including. This is not a book about stake holders, or creating a Ubiquitous Language for our Bounded Contexts. At least not yet... perhaps never.

It's strange... you'd think that as much money I've made for for so many people that I'd value their opinion more about these things, but the truth is, I don't. For me, the domain of a system is not the same thing as the utility of said domain. A well designed domain should be able to flower into an almost unlimited number of possible applications and perspectives. Stakeholders are thirsty, and thirsty, starving people aren't particularly creative. They want their value NOW. They don't care about quality. They could give a flying fuck about flexibility. They just had to sell their souls to a room full of sociopathic investors, and need to hit that sweet sweet startup lottery, baby!

They're junkies. Gambling junkies. And man, now that they're sniffing that A.I. crack, it's like 1983 all over again.

It's only recently that I realized it. I should have noticed sooner, growning up around musicians duing the final days of the world Freeway Ricky Ross built. (Trust me, metal heads and gangsta rappers got nothing on orchestral brass players. Don't let the Ravel fool ya.)

Having said that, I love you guys. Hey, late stage Capitalism is a hell of a drug. But I'm not getting high off my own supply. Let me cook. And when we cook, our muse is Telos. This is problem spelunking. Exploring a concept, and discovering how it realizes in digital form. It uses lenses of perspective, to give us windows into these ideas, and see how they manifest into practical tools and utilities. It's a journey, not a destination.

I started this because I like watching poker shows, and I was curious about all the statistics and graphics that they show, and about the poker solvers that they talk about. I wanted to take the game, and try to distill it down to it's essence, and then see how the types that make it up can be shaped into more complex things.

What makes games like poker particularly interesting is that, unlike games like chess, players only have access to a subset of the total information that makes up the game at any point in time.

In Chess, everything you know about the state of the game is right there in front of you:

What makes it a challenge, is how quickly the different possibilities can spiral out of human comprehension. The mathmetician Claude Shannon, OG computer chess programmer, described what is now called the Shannon Number, 10¹²⁰, which is a conservative lower bound of the game-tree complexity of chess. Now I am not a mathmetician, but that's a lot. Chess becomes a pure force of calculation on the part of the players, which of course creates nothing but very stable geniuses.

Poker, on the other hand, is a game where you only know what you can see, what you can sense, and what you can remember. What Game Theory calls, a game of incomplete information.

Now, I'm not telling you this because I want to flex on how smart I am. I'm telling you this as a hint to how stupid I am, and how as we go down this path, me and my big brain are going to do a lot of very time consuming shit, that would have been better spent cracking open a book, or talking to an expert. But what's the fun in that? Lord knows us comp sci guys are the masters of time and space and have already taken over the world, so why should we bother listening to anyone else?

There is not a more concentrated form of pure mischief than the deck of cards. Much like pasta, playing cards started in ancient China as a drinking game before spreading through India, Persia and Egypt, only to arrive in Europe in the late 14th century; the first record of which was their being banned in the city of Bern in 1367.

The modern deck that we play today in casinos is what is called the French Deck because of their choice of suits being Spades, Hearts, Diamonds and Clubs, as well as their introduction of the Queen. 🇫🇷 Vive la France! 🇫🇷

Deconstructing the Cards

In our quest to bring poker into The Matrix, we need to define what they are digitally.

If we were to define cards from a DDD perspective we would call them Value Objects. But we would also call their Ranks and Suits Value Objects, as well as their collection into a Deck or 52, or the hands each players are dealt, or the common cards sitting in the middle of the table. One of the funny things about talking with computer scientists is, given how specific their craft is, it sure can be fucking vague.

The truth is, that every domain you wrestle with has its own unique set of textures. In finance, it is the hell that is decimal places and percentages. In mail, it's the nightmare that is addresses and zip codes.

Playing cards are a fun one. I can't think of any domain where the underlying structures are so simple, and yet the results can be so complex. Maybe it's just that mathmeticians are all degenerate gamblers, but they've managed to make a simple deck of 52 cards a central player in the study in the fields of combinatorics, probability, information, game and group theory, as well as the study of the surprisingly tricky and soon to be very much in the news thanks to the fun that is quantum computing: randomness.
