τέλος

Thesis: Genius is complexity manifested from simplicity.

Why is Beethoven great? Why is his 5th Symphony in c minor his most famous work?1 A four-note theme, repeated twice. Dum dum dum dummm! Dum dum dum dummmmmmmm!

You can hear it in your head. It's like a psychic time machine. It's a sense memory that connects billions of people. Why?

What tools was Beethoven working with?

You had the Classical Style. 2 A formal harmonic and rhythmic structure, framed by a selection of contrasting movements honed by decades of tradition, founded by the dances of the royal courts, made alive by an innovative use of instruments to perform the music, served to an audience of the rising European middle class starving for novel forms of entertainment.

You had the manic Viennese composer/performer, hell-bent to pave his own way regardless of public perception, his isolation magnified by his gradual loss of hearing.

It couldn't have been the premier. That was a disaster. A barely rehearsed 4-hour-long concert, in the middle of a winter storm. Yet within a few years, it was considered a masterpiece.

Dum dum dum dummm! Dum dum dum dummmmmmmm!

So, what is it?

The secret to unlocking the pure genius of Beethoven's 5th is right there in the opening.

It's a ladder. V III IV II. 5 3 4 2 ____. We're in the ke of c minor. Start at the top: G or V. Subtract 2: III or Eb . Go up one: IV or F. Subtract 2 again: II or D. It's a formula... X - (2 + 1)...

Except something is missing. V III IV II... no I. No root of the chord. No home.

From the start, Beethoven pushes us into this manic journey. Through the movements we travel through an Odyssean struggle to find home...

  1. Allegro con brio in c minor
  2. Andante con moto in A♭ major
  3. Scherzo in c minor

Da da da dummm! Da da da dummmmmmmm!

I want to go home. This feeling is infused into the music from the most basic of elements to its largest architectural structure. Three movements of struggle, until we reach the end, and what do we find?

The C Major shores of home, beaten over our heads as only Beethoven can do.

(Is having just watched Christopher Nolan's film adaptation of The Odyssey helping?)

There is no escaping this. We have reached home. There is no subtlety in how Beethoven sends us the message. However, because it's a message of notes instead of words, while it's much harder to grab onto intellectually, it's almost impossible to ignore emotionally.

Scale up to the sweeping lines, or scale down to the tiniest details, they all serve the greater purpose of the essence of what this piece is about.

The Ancient Greeks called this Telos3: the essence of a thing. My thesis of this book is that genius is how elegantly complexity manifests itself from simplicity. Atoms becoming rocket ships. Subatomic particles becoming atoms. Chains of chemical information allowing for the creation of an infinite variety of life. A simple fungus occupying hundreds of miles.

Genius is simplicity manifesting into things of great beauty and power.

You can see the patterns everywhere.

The haystacks and water lilies of Monet. The urgent offbeat rhythmic drive echoing out across millennia until James Brown screams, "take it to the bridge!"4 The contrasting call and response of Party and Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan. The slowly accelerating mania of Lucy and Ethel wrapping chocolates. Any guitar solo by Sister Rosetta Tharpe.

They are the seeds of humanity. The link us across time and space. They are the living magic of telepathy and time travel that is consiousness.

Why Rust?

TBH, this is a bet. For a long time I've felt like the domain surface of complex software systems is much too wide, unorganized, and exposed. Now that we are living through the era of agentic coding solutions, we are seeing that risk manifest in real time. Agentic coding systems may not be the best at constructing architectural beauty, but they can sure as shit act like a million chaos monkeys. Organizations will have no choice but to tighten up everything.

When you get right down to it, Rust is a trauma response from the years and years of defects created by other languages. I am a firm believer in making problems impossible instead of traditional creating controls to prevent them. Rust can create shit systems with the best of them, but by its very design it makes a whole collection of code crimes simply impossible to commit.

Programming languages like JavaScript and Python were never designed to be the source of truth of complex systems. Suddenly, the velocity of code change is accelerating exponentially, and languages designed with flexibility in mind are seeing that feature being used against them. Anyone maintaining systems now is seeing that effect in real time. Security alerts about popular libraries are now a daily occurrence. Agentic coding systems may not be the best at constructing architectural beauty, but they can sure as shit act like a million chaos monkeys.

You see, when I talk abour failing as a skill, it's all about controlled failure. Minimizing risk. Python, and other scripting languages like it, are so flexible that the variance they introduce, compounded by the velocity of the coding agents that produce it, creates a terrifying cocktail. Add on to that the fact that many of the people using these systems have no fucking clue what the code they are creating actually does.

Rust, combined with disciplined design and technologies like web assembly, offer a way to simplify and organize what has been traditionally been a mess, outside of the exhalted chambers of bank mainframe system develoers. Not only do I believe that but I intend to prove it.

That doesn't mean that these languages like Python and JavaScript won't play a supporting role in this exercise. One of my favorite returns of investment in the way I have been coding, is how easily I can take my core library and wrap it in Python or Node.JS. How I can use that same single library and create not just one, but several web applications, all using a blob of code that's under 5MBs, and working with the same, battle tested library functioning as a single source of truth.

Rust strings still terrify me. I feel like I am a lot better at dealing with them then when I first started out, but still... damn do they call out all of my deficiencies as a developer. My php, perl coding early ass didn't know shit... didn't learn shit... was just making it up as I went along... ASIDE: I still am.

Basically, a String is is stored in the heap2 of your program. It's like that extra suitcase you take on a trip so that you can fit in silly nick knacks you collect along your way. A str primitive type, on the other hand, is something that you can borrow, but not buy. It's like that key to the gas station restroom, chained to an old license plate. You can borrow it, but it's not yours.

In this case, the hello__world function is returning &'static str; what's called a string literal. It's a string that's burned into your program, and will exist unti it ends. It's like that tattoo you got that one night, in that place were no one can see it... you know the one.

I can see three paths forward. Here's the simplest. I have a static function that's trying to return a static str, that insteads returning the results of a function that returns a String.

Question #2 Do the unit tests pass?

Regression testing (rarely, non-regression testing[1]) is re-running functional and non-functional tests to ensure that previously developed and tested software still performs as expected after a change.[2] If not, that would be called a regression.

/// Fetch edge features based on `input` struct into [`Features`] buffer.
pub fn fetch_features(
    &mut self,
    input: &dyn BotsInput,
    features: &mut Features,

    // update features checksum (lower 32 bits) and copy edge feature names
    features.checksum &= 0xFFFF_FFFF_0000_0000;
    features.checksum |= u64::from(self.config.checksum);
    let (feature_values, _) = features

char is not "the characters on a playing card". char is every Unicode scalar value, and the compiler will not let you pretend otherwise. It is not asking what a card is. It is asking what happens when someone hands you a 'z'.

    #[case('t', Rank::TEN)]
    #[case('0', Rank::TEN)]

    #[case('8', Rank::EIGHT)]
    #[case('7', Rank::SEVEN)]
    #[case('6', Rank::SIX)]
    #[case('5', Rank::FIVE)]
    #[case('4', Rank::FOUR)]
    #[case('3', Rank::TREY)]
    #[case('2', Rank::DEUCE)]
    #[case('_', Rank::BLANK)]
    #[case(' ', Rank::BLANK)]
    #[case('z', Rank::BLANK)]

        assert_eq!(expected, Rank::from(input));
    }
}

$> git checkout step-008-from-char-tested

Two conventions worth naming, because they run through the rest of this book.

The test is called from__char, with two underscores, which is not idiomatic Rust and is why the module allows non_snake_case. The doubled underscore reads as "the tests for from, the char ones". When a test fails at three in the morning, the name should tell you where to stand before you have read a line of its body.

And the cases are exhaustive on purpose — 'z' and ' ' and '_' sit in the same list as the ace. The negative cases are not an afterthought bolted on at the bottom. They are the reason the list exists at all. The compiler already made us answer for them once. The tests hold us to the answer.
